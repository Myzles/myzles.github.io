How to find decision makers at a company

The person who can say yes is rarely the person whose email is on the contact page. Here's how to find them by name and title, using free sources, and how to reach them once you have a name.

1. Work out who actually decides

Before you search, decide which title you're looking for:

  • Under about 20 people: the owner, founder or managing partner signs off on almost everything.
  • Professional firms (law, accounting, dental, agencies): partners, principals or the practice manager.
  • Larger companies: the head of the team that would use what you sell (VP Sales for a sales tool, IT director for software), plus whoever holds the budget.

2. Read the company's own website

Most small and mid-sized firms list their leaders. Try /team, /about, /about-us, /leadership, /people, /our-team, and for law firms /attorneys or /lawyers. Note the name, exact title and any email or direct phone next to them.

If the menu has nothing, search the site from Google: site:example.com (founder OR owner OR partner OR director).

3. Check public company registries

Registries list who legally runs a company, for free:

  • UK: Companies House search shows every director and officer, with appointment dates.
  • US: each state's Secretary of State business search usually shows officers or a registered agent. Coverage varies a lot by state.
  • EU: national business registers, and the site's own imprint page, which often names the managing director.

Registries are accurate on who's in charge, but they won't tell you who runs marketing.

4. Search LinkedIn and the news

  • On LinkedIn, search the company, open People, and filter by title keywords. A free account limits how many profiles you can see, but names and titles are usually enough. Look them up by hand: scraping LinkedIn breaks its terms.
  • Search news and press releases: "Example Ltd" (appointed OR hires OR promoted). New leaders get announced, and new leaders are the most open to new suppliers.
  • Conference speaker pages and podcast guest lists are good for department heads.

5. Get a contact route

  1. Use an email listed next to their name on the company's site if there is one. That's the only kind you can trust without checking.
  2. Otherwise, find the company's pattern from any published staff address (jane.doe@, jdoe@, jane@) and apply it. Treat it as a guess.
  3. Check the domain takes mail with nslookup -type=mx example.com. More on that in how to find email addresses on a website.
  4. A mutual connection or a reply to something they posted beats a cold email every time.

FAQ

How do I find the owner of a small business?

Check the about or team page, the imprint page, and the state or national company registry, which lists officers or directors for free.

How do I find out who makes purchasing decisions?

Match what you sell to the team that would use it, then find the head of that team on the company site, LinkedIn or recent press releases. At small firms it's usually the owner.

Can I trust a guessed email address?

No. A pattern like firstname.lastname@ is a guess until it's confirmed. Only addresses published by the company are reliable.
